Call for Project Proposals

BrainHack projects are what participants spend two days working on together. A project can be a tiny question or a large community tool, a vague idea or a released package. If it uses computation to tackle a problem, we'd love to have it.

What fits?

There is only one real rule

Your project should be computational and aim to solve a problem: writing or improving code, building a pipeline, analysing or organising data, developing a model or method, or helping people learn the tools to do any of these.

Beyond that, we are open to every size, stage and format. Most projects are about the brain, but past editions have also hosted gut-microbiome analyses and simulations of embryonic development. If you're unsure whether your idea fits, just ask us.

Any size, any stage

People often ask how big or how advanced a project should be. The honest answer is: anywhere on these scales is fine. Here are real projects from previous editions at different points on each.

Stage

How far along is it?
  • Just an idea, no code yet e.g. One Cell Wonder (2026) started from a concept: grow an organism from one cell with simple rules.
  • A prototype or basic structure e.g. EyePrep (2026) had a first structure and needed QC, tests and visualisations.
  • A released, mature tool e.g. SLAM, a published Python package that its team has improved at several BrainHacks.

Scale

How big is the goal and the team?
  • One focused question, a few people e.g. Bias correction of heterogeneous MRI images (2024): make one pipeline step robust to a specific artefact.
  • A lab-sized workflow e.g. Awake dog dMRI (2026): build a full preprocessing workflow for one dataset.
  • A community-wide effort e.g. NARPS Open Pipelines (2023): reproduce 70 fMRI analysis pipelines as an open resource.

Expertise needed

Who can join in?
  • No prior experience e.g. WISE (2024) helped people start Python from scratch, with no prerequisites.
  • Some coding or domain knowledge e.g. Surf(ac)ing fMRI data (2023) combined fMRIPrep, Workbench and nilearn; some Python recommended.
  • Advanced or specialist e.g. GPU porting of information-flow measures (2020) in the Frites package.

What makes a good proposal

You don't need everything polished; you can keep editing your proposal after submitting it. These points help participants pick your project and get started quickly.

  • A clear problemWhat are you trying to solve, for whom, and why does it matter?
  • Goals that fit two daysOne or two concrete things you'd be happy to have achieved by the end of the hacking days.
  • Good first issuesSmall, well-defined tasks newcomers can start on right away.
  • Skills you're looking forAnd what people can contribute even without them: testing, documentation and fresh eyes all count.
  • Data and resourcesWhat data or code people will work with, and how to get set up before the event.
  • What participants will learnA tool, a method, a dataset, a way of working: tell people what they'll take home.

How to submit

Fill in the form

Click Submit a project and complete the GitHub issue form. Dummy content is fine if you're missing something.

A project monitor checks your proposal and helps you shape it if needed.

Once approved, your project appears on the BrainHack Marseille 2027 website.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need to have code or results already?
No. Several past projects started as pure ideas (see One Cell Wonder or WISE). What matters is that you can describe the problem and are ready to minimally organise the work.
Is my project too small, or too big?
Probably neither. A single focused question with two or three people works well, and so does a chunk of a large community tool. For big projects, pick a part that can progress in two days.
Does it have to be about neuroscience?
Most projects are, but it isn't required. Past editions have included gut-microbiome analysis and simulations of embryonic development. The requirement is that the project is computational and aims to solve a problem.
Can my project be a tutorial or a learning session?
Yes. Projects such as WISE (learning Python from scratch) or the nipype/pydra project were built around helping others get hands-on with a tool.
Which programming languages and tools are OK?
Any. Python is the most common, but past projects have also used Matlab, ImageJ, FSL, JAX, web technologies and more.
Can I bring my own data?
Yes, many projects do. Make sure the data can be shared with your collaborators, or prepare a shareable sample or an open dataset for people to work on.
I don't have a GitHub account. Can I still submit?
Creating a GitHub account is free and takes a couple of minutes. If that's a blocker, email us at brainhack.marseille@gmail.com and we'll help you submit.
Can I submit with co-leaders, or lead more than one project?
Co-leading is welcome: the form has fields for leaders and collaborators. If you're thinking of leading several projects, get in touch so we can discuss it.
What is the deadline?
The submission deadline will be announced soon. Submitting early helps participants discover your project and prepare before the event.
